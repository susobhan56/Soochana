Age Structure: who is young, who is old

How many children, working-age adults and older people live in Odisha, and how that mix is changing.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 1991 and 2011; DIU population projection (Bayesian approach) for 2021–2036 · Every number is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

The age mix of a population shows how many people need support and how many can work, and it changes slowly over decades.

Population pyramid
A chart of people by age: men on one side, women on the other.
Age group
People of a similar age, such as 20–24, or all children aged 0–14.
Working age (15–59)
The ages when most people study, work or earn.
Dependency ratio
Children plus older people, for every 100 people of working age.
Ageing
When the share of older people in the population keeps rising.

Reading a pyramid

A population pyramid is two bar charts placed back to back. Each bar is one five-year age group. Babies (aged 0–4) are at the bottom. People aged 80 and over are at the top. Men are on the left and women are on the right.

The length of a bar shows how many people are in that group. We count in lakh (1 lakh = 1,00,000 people).

The shape tells a story. A wide base means many babies are being born. A narrowing base means fewer births. A bulge is a large group of people, moving up through the ages as they grow older.

If Odisha were 100 people

Crores of people are hard to picture. So imagine Odisha as a group of just 100 people.

Each square below is one person. Teal squares are children (0–14). Violet squares are people of working age (15–59). Orange squares are older people (60 and over).

How many people depend on each earner?

Children and most older people depend on others. Someone has to grow the food, earn the money and pay for schools and health care. Most of that work is done by people aged 15 to 59.

The dependency ratio compares these groups. It counts the children and older people for every 100 people of working age. A lower number means fewer dependants for each earner.

( children 0–14 (lakh) + older people 60+ (lakh) ) ÷ working age 15–59 (lakh) × 100to say "per 100" = dependency ratio,

The ratio has two parts. The child ratio counts only children per 100 workers. The old-age ratio counts only people aged 60 and over. Add them and you get the total.

The "demographic window" Of the six years shown, 2021 has the largest share of people of working age (%). When many people can work and fairly few depend on them, a state has a chance to grow faster. Economists call this a demographic window (or demographic dividend). It only pays off if young people get good schooling, health care and jobs. And it does not stay open for ever: as today's young adults grow old, the share of older people keeps rising.

Districts age differently

Not every district has the same shape. Some have many children. Others have more older people. This can happen when families have had fewer children for longer, or when young adults move away for work.

Districts differ a lot in size. So here we compare shares: each bar is the percentage of that district's own people in the age group. The black outline is Odisha as a whole.

Remember

  1. A population pyramid shows people by age: men on the left, women on the right, babies at the bottom.
  2. Odisha's pyramid is narrowing at the base and widening at the top: fewer children, more older people.
  3. The dependency ratio counts children and older people for every 100 people aged 15–59. It fell from in 1991 to in 2021, and is projected to rise again as Odisha ages.

Data and method

1991 and 2011 are Census of India counts by five-year age group and sex. People whose age was not stated are left out of the age groups, so the pyramid totals ( lakh in 1991, lakh in 2011) are slightly below the full census populations. 2021, 2026, 2031 and 2036 are from the DIU population projection (Bayesian approach) used across Soochana; the district pyramids come from the same projection. See Projecting the future for how these are made.

Broad groups: children 0–14, working age 15–59, older people 60 and over. This follows the usual Indian convention; many international sources use 15–64 instead, which gives a different ratio. Dependency ratio = (0–14 + 60+) ÷ (15–59) × 100. Child ratio = (0–14) ÷ (15–59) × 100; old-age ratio = (60+) ÷ (15–59) × 100. The 100-people picture rounds each share with the largest-remainder method, so the three numbers always add to 100.
