Train, Test and Validation

A model that has already seen the answers will always look clever, so we test it on districts it has never seen.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011, NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

Never grade a model on the districts it learned from: keep some hidden until the end.

Can we guess how many young children are stunted (too short for their age) in a district? We try to guess it from one thing we already know: how many women can read.

Mothers who can read tend to have healthier, better-fed children. So female literacy is a good clue.

Teaching a model with 30 districts is easy. The hard part is checking it honestly. Will it work on a district it has never seen?

Thirty districts

Each dot is one district. Left to right: the share of women who could read in 2011. Bottom to top: the share of children under five who were stunted in NFHS-5 (2019–21).

The pattern is clear. Where more women read, fewer children are stunted.

Grading its own homework

Let's fit a very bendy curve to all 30 districts. (It is a degree- polynomial: a higher degree lets a curve bend more.) Then we check it on the same 30 districts.

Its typical miss is percentage points. A straight line misses by . Graded this way, the bendy curve wins.

But look at the ends, where it shoots off the chart. It has learned the quirks of these 30 districts, not the real pattern.

Hide some answers

The fix is to hide some districts before the model learns. We shuffle the 30 and deal them into three piles:

Training (18) to teach the model. Validation (6) to choose between models. Test (6), locked away until the very end.

Test, once

Finally we open the test pile and score the chosen model, just once.

It misses by points on these six districts. This is the number to report. Nothing about the model was tuned to them.

Why three piles, not two?

We picked the winner by looking at its validation score. So the winner is partly tuned to those six districts, and that score flatters it.

The test pile played no part in any choice. Its score is still honest.

Does it matter which districts land in the test pile?

Yes, a lot. Six test districts is a small sample.

Say the shuffle puts Angul, Jajpur and Rayagada in the test pile. Each sits almost exactly on the trend, so the model looks brilliant. Put Sambalpur, Puri or Bhadrak there instead (each 8 or more points off the trend), and the same model looks poor.

Below, we deal the districts 1,000 times. Each time, we teach a straight line on the training pile and score it on the test pile.

One model, scored on 1,000 different deals

Each bar counts deals that gave that score. A wide spread means the score depends on luck.

Cross-validation is a fairer way to score. Split the 30 districts into five groups of six (called folds). Teach the model on four groups and test it on the fifth. Do this five times, so every district is tested once. Then take the average of the five scores.

Now switch the chart above to 5-fold cross-validation. The bars bunch up much more tightly. Each score now uses all 30 districts, not just six lucky or unlucky ones. With small data like ours, this is the fair default.

What if we need to predict the next survey?

Random piles assume new districts look like the old ones. But often we want to predict the next survey.

Remember

  1. A score on districts the model learned from proves nothing. Always test it on districts it has not seen.
  2. Use the validation pile (or cross-validation) to make choices. Open the test pile only once, at the end.
  3. With only 30 districts, one deal is partly luck, so cross-validation is fairer. If the model must predict the next survey, test it on a later survey.

Data and method

Female literacy is from Census 2011. Stunting (children under 5, height-for-age) is from the NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 district fact sheets, as compiled in the portal's Health workbook.

The "typical miss" is the root-mean-square error (RMSE): square each district's miss, average the squares, then take the square root. It is in percentage points. Curves are polynomials fitted by least squares, with the literacy axis rescaled to −1…1 for numerical stability. A degree-d polynomial can bend up to d − 1 times.

Random deals use a fixed seed, so every reader sees the same first split; Deal again draws a new one. The luck chart deals 24 training and 6 test districts each time; the "spread" is the standard deviation of the 1,000 scores. Cross-validation uses 5 folds of 6 districts.

The models are teaching devices. Thirty districts are far too few for a planning tool, and literacy is not the only reason stunting differs.
