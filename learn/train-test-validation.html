Train, Test and Validation

A model that has seen the answers will always look clever. Here is how to find out whether it has actually learned something, using child stunting across Odisha's 30 districts.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011, NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser

Suppose the state wants a quick way to estimate how many children under five are stunted (too short for their age) in a district, from something already known about it. Female literacy is a natural candidate: mothers who can read tend to have better-fed, healthier children.

We can fit a model to the 30 districts and see how well it predicts. The hard part is not fitting it. It is measuring honestly how well it will work on a district it has not seen.

Thirty districts

Each dot is one district. Across: the share of women who could read in 2011. Up: the share of children under five who were stunted in NFHS-5 (2019–21).

The pattern is clear. Where more women read, fewer children are stunted.

Fit, and grade yourself

Let's fit a very flexible curve (a degree- polynomial) to all 30 districts, then check its predictions on those same districts.

Its typical error is percentage points, better than a straight line's on the same districts. Graded this way, the wiggly curve wins.

But look at the ends, where it shoots off the chart. It has fitted the quirks of these 30 districts, not the pattern.

Hide some answers

The fix is to set some districts aside before training. We shuffle the 30 and deal them into three piles:

Training (18) to fit the model, validation (6) to choose between models, and test (6), locked away until the very end.

Back on the chart

Same districts, same positions, now coloured by pile. The model may learn only from the training dots.

Train

We fit three candidates to the 18 training districts: a straight line, a gentle curve (degree 3) and a wiggly one (degree 8).

On training data the wiggliest always wins. Training errors: , and points.

Validate

Now score each candidate on the 6 validation districts, which none of them saw.

Validation errors: , and . The does best on districts it has not seen, so that is the model we keep.

Test, once

Finally we unlock the test districts and score the chosen model on them once.

Its error on them is points. That is the number to report, because nothing about the model was tuned to these six districts.

Why three piles, not two?

We chose between the candidates by looking at validation scores, so the winner is partly tuned to those six districts and its validation score flatters it.

The test pile had no say in any choice, so its score is still an honest one.

With 30 districts, the dealing is luck

Six test districts is a small sample. If the shuffle happens to put Angul, Jajpur and Rayagada in the test pile (each sits almost exactly on the trend), the model looks brilliant. Put Sambalpur, Puri or Bhadrak there instead (each 8 or more points off it) and the same model looks poor.

Below we deal the districts 1,000 times, fit a straight line on each training pile, and record the test error. Each dot in the strip is one deal.

Cross-validation uses every district for testing once. Split the 30 into five folds of six. Train on four folds and test on the fifth, rotating until each fold has been the test set. Then average the five scores.

Switch the strip above to 5-fold cross-validation. The spread of estimates shrinks, because each estimate now averages over all 30 districts instead of whichever 6 happened to land in the test pile. On small data like ours, this is the honest default.

When the test comes from the future

Random piles assume the districts we will predict look like the ones we trained on. Often we want to predict the next survey instead. Here we fit a line of stunting against female literacy on NFHS-4 (2015–16), then use it to predict NFHS-5 (2019–21).

NFHS-4, used for training NFHS-5, the future test Line fitted on NFHS-4

Take-aways A score on data the model trained on is not evidence. Keep a test set that plays no part in any decision. Use a validation set, or cross-validation, to make the decisions. With only 30 districts, a single split is closer to a lottery ticket than a measurement. And if the model is meant for the next survey, test it on a later survey, not on a random pile.

Data and method

Female literacy is from Census 2011; stunting (children under 5, height-for-age) is from the NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 district fact sheets as compiled in the portal's Health workbook. Errors are root-mean-square errors in percentage points. Polynomials are fitted by least squares with the literacy axis rescaled to −1…1 for numerical stability. Random deals use a fixed seed, so every reader sees the same first split. Deal again draws a new one. The models are teaching devices: 30 districts are far too few for a planning tool, and literacy is not the only reason stunting differs.
