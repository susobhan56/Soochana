The Bias–Variance Tradeoff

A model can be wrong by being too simple or too wiggly, and the best one sits in between.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

Too simple misses the pattern, too flexible chases noise: the best model balances the two.

Bias
Built-in error from a model too simple to see the pattern.
Variance
How much a model's answer jumps when the data change a little.
Underfitting
Too simple: high bias. Misses the pattern even on known districts.
Overfitting
Too flexible: high variance. Learns the noise, fails on new districts.
Model complexity
How flexible a model is: how much it can bend.

Can we guess how many young children are underweight (too light for their age) in a district? We try to guess it from one thing we already know: the share of people who belong to a Scheduled Tribe.

In Odisha the two go together. Districts with more tribal people tend to be more remote, with more forest villages and fewer nearby health services.

Tribal share does not cause underweight. It is a marker for many things at once. But it is a good test case. The link is not a straight line, and the districts do not sit neatly on any curve. That is when a model can go wrong in two opposite ways.

Thirty districts

Each dot is one district. Left to right: the share of people who are Scheduled Tribe (Census 2011). Bottom to top: the share of children under five who are underweight (NFHS-5, 2019–21).

Underweight rises steeply across the least tribal districts, then much more gently. And many districts sit well above or below their neighbours.

Too simple: one number

The simplest model ignores tribal share. It guesses the same figure for every district: the average, %.

Its typical miss is percentage points. This is not bad luck. The model is built to miss, because it cannot see the pattern at all. This built-in error is called bias.

A straight line does much better: a typical miss of points. But its misses follow a pattern.

A line cannot bend. It will miss in the same places, however much data we give it. That is bias too.

Too flexible: a degree-10 curve

Now let the curve bend a lot. A degree-10 curve (it can bend up to nine times) has a typical miss of only points on these 30 districts.

But look at the wiggles between districts, and the plunge at each end. It has learned these 30 districts, quirks and all, not the pattern.

What if the survey had come out differently?

Each district's figure comes from a sample survey. Survey different households and every dot would move a little. A good model should not change much.

We can imitate this with a redraw (statisticians call it a bootstrap). Pick 30 districts at random, allowing repeats. In this redraw, districts were left out and were picked more than once. Refit the degree-10 curve and it changes shape completely.

Fifty redraws: the straight line

Now do that fifty times. Here is the straight line refitted on each redraw.

The lines form a tight bundle. A typical guess moves by only about points between redraws. How much a model jumps around when the data change is called variance. The line's variance is low.

The same fifty redraws, now with the degree-10 curve. The bundle explodes into a fan.

A typical guess swings by about points between redraws. Yet the real figures only run from % to %. This model has very high variance. Its answer depends more on which districts it saw than on the pattern.

Bias, made visible

Nobody knows the true pattern. As a stand-in, we use a smooth average of nearby districts (the dashed line).

Now average the fifty straight lines. The result still misses the stand-in: too high on the left, too low in the middle, wrong again on the right. That average gap, about points, is the line's bias. More redraws will not shrink it.

Just right: degree

A gentle curve (degree ) sits in between. Its average stays within about points of the pattern. The line was points away.

Its bundle also stays fairly tight: a typical swing of points, nowhere near the degree-10 fan. Of all the degrees, this one has the smallest total error. Finding that balance is the bias–variance tradeoff.

Where does a model's error come from?

The error a model makes on a new district has three parts. They add up exactly:

expected error = bias² + variance + noise

Bias² is how far the model's average curve sits from the pattern. Variance is how much the model jumps around from one redraw to the next. Noise is everything tribal share cannot explain. Even a perfect curve would miss by this much.

Here, noise comes to about squared points. That is a typical miss of points that no model using only tribal share can remove.

Why "squared"? Each miss is multiplied by itself first, because that is how the three parts add up. To get back to a typical miss in points, take the square root of the total.

Can we keep a flexible model calm?

Choosing fewer bends is not the only fix. Ridge regression keeps all the bends of the degree-10 curve. But it adds a penalty for sharp bends. The size of the penalty is set by λ (the Greek letter lambda).

With no penalty, you get the wild fan from before. As the penalty grows, the fan closes. Too much penalty squashes the curve flat towards the average, and bias comes back.

Remember

  1. A model that is too simple is wrong in the same way every time. That is bias, and more data will not fix it.
  2. A model that is too flexible is wrong in a different way every time. That is variance. It is worst when data are few, like our 30 districts.
  3. Find the balance by testing on districts the model did not see (as in explainer 01), never by how well it fits the districts it learned from.

Data and method

Each redraw is a bootstrap resample: 30 districts drawn at random with replacement. All error figures average over the same redraws (fixed seed), so every reader sees the same charts; Redraw districts only changes the forty curves on display. Polynomials are fitted by least squares with the tribal-share axis rescaled to −1…1; ridge adds the penalty λ to the squared coefficients on that scale.

Because the true pattern is unknown, bias needs a stand-in. We use a local linear smoother: at each point it fits a line to nearby districts, weighting the closest most. Its width ( percentage points of tribal share) was chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation. Noise is the smoother's leftover scatter, corrected for its flexibility: squared points. A check that needs no model at all, comparing districts that sit next to each other on the tribal-share axis, gives . Bias² is the average, over the 30 districts, of the squared gap between the average fitted curve and the stand-in; variance is the average spread of the fits around their own average. Their sum with noise is the expected squared error on a fresh district, if the stand-in were the truth.

Caveats. Thirty districts are far too few for a planning tool, and district figures are survey estimates with their own sampling error. Tribal share is a marker for remoteness, poverty and access to services, not a cause of underweight. The models here are teaching devices.
