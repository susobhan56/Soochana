Where the numbers come from

Every number on Soochana was counted, asked or written down by someone, and here is how.

By DIU Team · Data: Census of India, NFHS-4, NFHS-5, SRS, UDISE+ · Charts are computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

Soochana's numbers are either counted (Census), estimated from a sample (NFHS) or recorded as they happen (SRS).

Census
A count of every person in the country, every 10 years.
Sample
A small group picked to stand in for everyone.
Survey
Asking questions to a sample of people or households.
Estimate
A careful best guess worked out from a sample.
Margin of error
How far an estimate may sit from the true value, by chance.

Who collects what?

Soochana brings together numbers from four main sources. Each one collects data in a different way. Each one is good at different things.

The Census is complete but comes rarely. NFHS goes deep into health but asks only a sample. SRS is the best source for births and deaths, but only for the state as a whole. UDISE+ comes from school records.

Why a sample works (and where it wobbles)

NFHS does not measure every child in a district. It measures a sample. Can a few hundred children tell us about all of them? Mostly yes, but each sample gives a slightly different answer.

Below is a pretend district of 1,000 children. The share who are stunted (too short for their age) is set to the real NFHS-5 value for the district you pick. A faint orange square is a stunted child. A real survey cannot see these colours. It has to ask.

True shares: NFHS-5 (2019–21) district stunting, children under 5. The 1,000 children and the samples are a simulation.

Look at the three rows. With 50 children, the dots scatter widely. With 800, they huddle close to the true line. Bigger samples wobble less.

The band shows the margin of error. Statisticians work it out with a short formula: 1.96 × √( p × (1 − p) ÷ n ), where p is the share and n is the number asked. You do not need to remember it. Just remember the idea: four times the sample halves the wobble.

Is a change real?

Every survey round has its own wobble. So when a district's number moves between two rounds, part of the move may be chance. Before saying "stunting rose in this district", we should ask: is the change bigger than the wobble?

The chart compares stunting in NFHS-4 (2015–16) and NFHS-5 (2019–21) for all 30 districts. The grey band around each NFHS-4 dot is the wobble zone. If the NFHS-5 dot lands inside it, the change could be just chance.

A word of honesty. We do not have the exact number of children measured in each district, so the wobble zone here is approximate. Real surveys visit households in clusters (whole villages at a time), which makes the true wobble a little wider than our band. Treat the grey rows as "not sure", not as "no change".

Remember

  1. The Census counts everyone, but only every 10 years. Population numbers after 2011 are projections.
  2. Survey numbers (like NFHS) are estimates from a sample. They have a margin of error, and bigger samples wobble less.
  3. Before calling a change real, check that it is bigger than the wobble of both rounds.

Data and method

Sources. Literacy: Census of India 2011. TFR: NFHS-5 (2019–21) state fact sheet. IMR: SRS Statistical Report 2024. Secondary dropout: UDISE+ 2024–25. District stunting (height-for-age below −2 SD, children under 5): NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 district fact sheets, as compiled in the portal's Health workbook.

Sampling demo. The district of 1,000 children is a simulation: the number of stunted children is set to the district's NFHS-5 share, placed at random. Each button press draws a simple random sample without replacement (seeded, so every reader starts with the same sequence). The band is the usual 95% margin for a proportion, ±1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n). NFHS itself uses a stratified two-stage cluster sample with survey weights, so its real margins are somewhat wider than a simple random sample of the same size.

Is a change real? District sample sizes are not in the portal's data, so the number of children measured is an assumption you can change (250, 500 or 1,000 per round). The wobble zone is ±1.96 × √(p₄(1−p₄)/n + p₅(1−p₅)/n), the approximate 95% margin for the difference between two independent rounds. It ignores the cluster design effect, so it is, if anything, too narrow.
