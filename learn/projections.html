Projecting the future: how we estimate 2036

Nobody has counted Odisha's people in 2036, so here is how Soochana's numbers for 2021 to 2036 are worked out.

By DIU Team · Data: Census of India; DIU population and fertility projections (Bayesian approach) · Every number is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

Pictures in this box are illustrations, not real data.

A projection moves today's population forward five years at a time, using careful guesses about births, deaths and migration.

Projection
An estimate of the future, if present trends continue.
Cohort
A group of people born in the same years, followed as they age.
Cohort-component method
Age each cohort forward, remove deaths, add births, adjust for migration.
Bayesian approach
Combine past patterns from many places with local data to get many possible futures.
Uncertainty range
The spread of believable values around the middle estimate.

The orange bars below are one cohort: a group of people born in the same five years. In 2021 they are aged 10–14.

Press Next 5 years. The whole population gets five years older. Our cohort moves up one row. A new group of babies appears at the bottom, in teal.

These are Soochana's real projected numbers for Odisha. Watch how the size of the cohort changes, or does not.

The cohort gets smaller for two reasons. Some of its members die. And some move, out of Odisha or into it. The difference between two steps is deaths plus net migration.

Try the three buttons. People aged 10–14 hardly shrink at all, because few teenagers die. People aged 60–64 in 2021 shrink a lot: about in every 100 are gone by 2036.

The three ingredients

Every step of a projection needs three careful guesses. Each one comes from real data about the past.

Births ← fertility

How many babies?

This uses the fertility rate (TFR): how many children a woman is expected to have. It is based on NFHS surveys and the recent trend.

How many people die?

Migration

Who moves in or out?

This uses past patterns of people moving, such as Census migration tables. It is often the hardest part to guess.

Many possible futures: the Bayesian idea

Nobody knows exactly how many children women in Koraput will have in 2036. A single guess would hide that.

The Bayesian approach does something better. It starts with what we know from many places: in most districts and countries, fertility falls in a similar way over time. Then it adds this district's own survey data. Putting the two together, it produces thousands of possible futures. Each one is a believable path.

Soochana reports the middle path. The spread of the paths shows how sure we can be. The further ahead we look, the wider the spread.

A projection is not a promise

People often read a projection as a prediction. It is not quite the same thing.

MythA projection tells us exactly what will happen.

FactIt tells us what will happen if today's trends continue. It is a careful "if", not a promise.

MythIf the 2036 number turns out different, the method failed.

FactProjections are updated when new data arrive, such as a new census or NFHS round. Updating is part of the method.

MythNumbers for the future are made up.

FactThey are built from real counts (Census), real surveys (NFHS, SRS) and clear rules for births, deaths and migration.

Put everything together and you get the line below. Up to 2011, every point is a Census count. There has been no census count since 2011, so from 2021 the line is dashed: these are projections.

Remember

  1. A projection moves everyone forward five years at a time: remove expected deaths, add expected births, allow for migration. Then repeat.
  2. The Bayesian approach draws many possible futures and reports the middle one. The range shows how uncertain it is, and it gets wider the further ahead we look.
  3. A projection says what happens if trends continue. It is not a promise, and it is updated when new census or survey data arrive.

Data and method

Population and age groups, 2021–2036: DIU population projection (Bayesian approach), as used across Soochana; state figures follow the Census of India / ORGI population projections using the Bayesian approach. 1901–2011: Census of India. A 1936 row in the source file is not a census year and is left out of the line chart. District fertility: 2020 is computed from NFHS-5 (2019–21); 2021–2036 is the DIU district projection (Bayesian approach). The file's NFHS-4 column is not used.

The cohort-component method described here is the standard way such projections are built. The exact assumptions for fertility, mortality and migration are set out by ORGI and DIU; the sources named in the three cards are the usual ones. The grey paths in the fan chart are a teaching illustration only: seeded random walks (each year adds a random step with a standard deviation of 0.035 births per woman) centred on the published path. They are not draws from the actual model and not its published intervals. The cohort numbers include both sexes; the 80+ group is open-ended.
