Equality of Odds

A model can serve one group of districts worse than another without ever seeing the groups, so we check its mistakes group by group.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011, NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

Equality of odds asks that needy districts have the same chance of being picked in every group, and that districts not in need have the same chance of a false alarm.

Suppose the state has money for an extra child-nutrition programme. It wants the money to reach the districts that need it. We call a district in need if the share of its children under five who are stunted (too short for their age) is at or above the Odisha average of in NFHS-5 (2019–21).

The problem: survey results arrive years late. So planners give each district a score from data they already have. Every district whose score clears a bar gets the money. We will build such a score. Then we ask a question that overall accuracy cannot answer: does it work equally well for every kind of district?

We compare two groups. The first is the districts where at least 30% of people belong to Scheduled Tribes (ST). The second is the other . We call them higher-ST districts and other districts. The groups are just an example. The point is that a model can treat them differently even though it never sees which group a district is in.

Three ways to define “fair”

Equal share funded (also called demographic parity): fund the same share of each group. Now against .

Equal opportunity: a needy district has the same chance of funding in either group. That means equal true-positive rates. Now against .

Equality of odds (also called equalised odds): equal true-positive rates and equal false-positive rates. The false-positive rates are against .

One bar for everyone meets none of the three.

Why can't we have all three?

The share funded in a group is made of two parts: the needy districts funded, plus the districts not in need that are funded anyway.

Suppose both rates were equal in the two groups. The share funded would still differ, because need is more common in one group ( against ). It could match only if the score were useless and funded needy and non-needy districts at the same rate. So we must choose which kind of fairness matters most.

Why do the rates jump in big steps?

Real targeting would use blocks or villages, with hundreds of units in each group. It would also show how uncertain each rate is. NFHS stunting is itself a survey estimate: of the 30 districts sit within 2 points of the line. A slightly different survey sample could move them across it.

So which kind of fair should we choose?

No formula can settle this. Each definition protects against a different harm.

Equal opportunity asks that needy children have the same chance of help wherever they live. Equality of odds adds that districts not in need are funded equally often too, so no group gets more than its share of spare money. Equal share funded ignores need altogether. That makes little sense for a programme meant to follow need.

For a nutrition programme, missing a needy district is usually the worse mistake. That points to equal opportunity. Its price here is money spent in districts that did not need it. Setting a different bar for each group also uses group membership. That is a policy and legal question, not a statistical one.

Often the better fix is a better score. The gaps exist because female literacy and past stunting track need well in one group and less well in the other. Pick one needy and one non-needy higher-ST district at random: the score ranks the needy one higher of the time. Among the other districts, it does so only of the time. A score built on facts that track need equally well in every group closes the gap without trading one harm for another.

Remember

  1. Leaving group membership out of a model does not make it fair. Other inputs can carry the same pattern.
  2. Check the mistakes group by group: needy districts missed and false alarms, not just overall accuracy.
  3. When need is more common in one group, the different ideas of fairness cannot all hold. Decide which harm matters most, and count the cost in districts.

Data and method

A district is in need if NFHS-5 child stunting (under 5, height-for-age) is at or above the Odisha average given in the state fact sheet. Higher-ST districts are those with a Scheduled Tribe share of 30% or more in Census 2011. The need score is a logistic regression on female literacy (Census 2011) and NFHS-4 stunting, both standardised, trained by gradient descent (learning rate 0.5, 2,000 steps, no penalty). Each district's score comes from a model trained on the other districts (leave-one-out), so the error rates are not flattered by the model having seen the answer. A district is funded if its score is at or above its group's bar.

Why the definitions conflict: in any group, share funded = base rate × true-positive rate + (1 − base rate) × false-positive rate. If both rates match across groups but the base rates differ, the shares funded can match only when the two rates are equal to each other, that is, when the score does no better than chance. On the ROC chart, each bar is an operating point; exact equalised odds needs an operating point the two groups share.

The preset buttons try every pair of bars that give each group a different outcome, leaving out pairs that fund everyone or no one in a group. They pick the pair with the smallest gap on the chosen measure (for equalised odds, the larger of the two gaps), then the fewest wrong decisions, then the fewest needy districts missed. Research papers sometimes close the remaining gap by randomly mixing two bars for one group; we leave that out, since a lottery is hard to defend for public funding. The probability that the score ranks a random needy district above a random non-needy one is the area under each group's ROC curve (AUC). With districts, this is a teaching example, not a targeting tool.
