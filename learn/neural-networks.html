Neural Networks

A neural network is a set of small logistic regressions wired together. Here is how the wiring lets it draw curved boundaries, how it learns, and why, with only 30 districts, a bigger network is not a better one.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser

In the first explainer we predicted how many young children are stunted in a district. Here we ask a yes-or-no question instead: is a district's child stunting above the state average of %? In NFHS-5, of the 30 districts were at or above it.

We give the model two facts about each district, both from Census 2011: the share of women who can read, and the share of people who live in towns. Two inputs means we can draw everything the model does on one flat map.

One neuron

The smallest network is a single neuron. It multiplies each input by a weight, adds a constant (the bias), and squashes the total into a chance between 0 and 1:

Both inputs are first rescaled so that 0 means "an average district" and 1 means "one standard deviation above it". The weights are learned from the data.

The background shows its answer everywhere on the map: orange where it says "above average", teal where it says "below", grey where it is unsure. One neuron is exactly logistic regression (explainer 03).

A straight boundary

The line where the chance crosses 50% is the model's decision boundary. For a single neuron it is always a straight line, whatever the data look like.

Why the squash matters

The squashing step is called an activation function. Networks use a few kinds: the S-shaped sigmoid (0 to 1), its cousin tanh (−1 to 1), and ReLU, which turns negatives into zero.

The bend is the point. Without it, neurons stacked on neurons add up to one big weighted sum, and a weighted sum can only ever draw a straight line.

A hidden layer

Now put three neurons between the inputs and the output. Each is its own small logistic regression with its own weights (using tanh), so each draws its own straight line across the map: the dashed lines A, B and C.

The small squares in the diagram show each neuron's output across the whole map. They are called hidden neurons because nobody checks their outputs against the data. Only the final answer is graded.

Lines combine into a curve

The output neuron takes a weighted sum of the three hidden outputs and squashes it once more. Three steps, facing different ways, add up to a boundary that can bend.

This network gets of 30 right, against for the single neuron.

How it learns

Nobody sets the weights by hand. Training starts from small random weights, then nudges every weight a little in the direction that lowers the log-loss, a score that punishes confident wrong answers hardest.

Working out that direction, layer by layer from the output back to the inputs, is called backpropagation. Here every district is used in every nudge, so each nudge is one epoch. Watch of them.

More neurons, more wiggle

Give the hidden layer eight neurons and train again. It now gets of 30 right, and its log-loss falls from to . The boundary bends around single districts.

But it is marking its own homework. Whether that bending is real pattern or memorised noise, only districts it has not seen can tell (see The Bias–Variance Tradeoff).

Try it: build and train your own

Choose how many hidden layers and neurons the network has, which activation it uses and how big each nudge is (the learning rate). Then press Play and watch it learn. The ringed districts are the ones it currently gets wrong.

Things to try: No hidden layer can only draw a straight line. Eight neurons will eventually get almost every district right. A learning rate of 0.3 lurches about; 0.003 crawls. New random start trains the same network from different starting weights, and often finds a different boundary.

Is the bigger network better?

Training accuracy can only tell us how well a network remembers. To see how it does on districts it has not seen, we use 5-fold cross-validation (explainer 01): train on 24 districts, test on the other 6, rotate until every district has been tested once, then repeat the whole thing with three different shuffles.

Training the networks…

Take-aways A neuron is a weighted sum passed through an activation function; on its own it is logistic regression and draws a straight boundary. A hidden layer of neurons draws several lines, and the output neuron blends them into a curve. Training nudges every weight downhill on the log-loss, using backpropagation to find the direction. More neurons always fit the training districts better, but with 30 districts most of that extra flexibility fits noise. Judge a network on districts it has not seen. Neural networks earn their keep with thousands of examples, not thirty.

Data and method

Female literacy and urban population share are from Census 2011; the share of children under 5 who are stunted (too short for their age) is from the NFHS-5 district fact sheets (2019–21), as compiled in the portal's Health workbook. A district counts as "above average" when its stunting is at or above the NFHS-5 state figure for Odisha. Both inputs are z-scored (mean 0, standard deviation 1; for cross-validation, using the training districts only). Networks are fully connected, with a sigmoid output, trained on all training districts at once (full batch) with the Adam optimiser on log-loss, learning rate 0.03 and a fixed random seed unless you change them, so every reader sees the same first view. The story networks and the cross-validation use epochs and tanh. Cross-validation accuracy counts each held-out prediction once per shuffle. These models are teaching devices: 30 districts are far too few to train a neural network for real use, two inputs leave out most of what drives stunting, and a pattern across districts is a correlation, not a cause.
