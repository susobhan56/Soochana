Decision Trees

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes-or-no questions learned from data. Here is how one grows, question by question, to sort Odisha's 30 districts by child mortality, and why letting it grow too far is a trap.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser

Under-five mortality counts the children who die before their fifth birthday for every 1,000 born alive. In NFHS-5 (2019–21) it varies widely across Odisha's districts. Half of them sit above per 1,000 and half at or below it.

Suppose we want a simple rule that sorts districts into the high half and the low half, using two things already known about each one: the share of women who could read in 2011, and the share of people from Scheduled Tribes. A decision tree finds such a rule by asking one yes-or-no question at a time.

A game of twenty questions

In the game Twenty Questions, a good question splits the possibilities as cleanly as possible. A decision tree plays the same game with data. It asks something like “Is female literacy at or below 60%?”, sends each district left (yes) or right (no), and then asks again inside each group.

It starts with all districts in one box: high and low. Guessing “high” for every district would be right only of the time.

Scoring a question

Which question is best? Slide a dividing line across female literacy, and at each position measure how mixed the two sides are.

The yardstick is Gini impurity: the chance that two districts drawn at random from a group (with replacement) carry different labels. A pure group scores 0; a 50–50 group scores 0.5. The two sides are averaged, weighted by size. Lower is better.

Deeper still

At depth 3 the tree has leaves (the boxes at the bottom, which make the predictions) and sorts of the districts correctly.

An extra level can never lower the score on the districts the tree learned from. It can only keep it or raise it.

Grow until every box is pure

Keep splitting until no box mixes high and low. That takes levels and leaves, and the tree now sorts of these districts correctly.

But look at the rules it needed. of its leaves hold a single district. The tree has not found a pattern. It has memorised a list.

Is it any good?

Scoring a tree on the districts it grew from proves nothing (see Train, Test and Validation). So we use 5-fold cross-validation: grow the tree on four-fifths of the districts, test it on the fifth it never saw, rotate, and repeat for different shuffles.

Two simple brakes stop a tree from memorising. A maximum depth limits how many questions it may ask in a row. A minimum leaf size forbids answers based on one or two districts. Setting either is called pruning (strictly, pre-pruning), and the right setting is chosen the honest way: by cross-validation, not by training accuracy.

One tree is a shaky witness

This jumpiness is the reason random forests exist. Grow hundreds of trees, each on its own bootstrap sample and each allowed to consider only a random subset of features at every split, then let them vote. The quirks of individual trees tend to cancel out, and the vote is far steadier than any single tree.

Take-aways A decision tree learns a flowchart: at each step it picks the yes-or-no question that leaves the two groups least mixed. Its rules are easy to read and to explain, which is its great strength. Left alone it keeps splitting until it has memorised the data, so its training accuracy means nothing. Cap its depth or its leaf size, and choose the cap by cross-validation. And because one tree changes shape when a single district moves, treat any one tree's rules as a sketch, not a finding.

Data and method

Under-five mortality (deaths before age five per 1,000 live births) is from the NFHS-5 district fact sheets (2019–21); a district counts as high if its rate is above the median of the 30 districts, computed live. Female literacy, Scheduled Tribe share, urban share and population density are from Census 2011; hover a column heading in the data table for each feature's source. Trees are grown with CART: every split is a single “feature ≤ threshold” question, thresholds sit halfway between neighbouring district values, and the split chosen is the one with the lowest size-weighted Gini impurity (or entropy, where selected). A leaf predicts its majority label; an even split is broken towards “high”. Cross-validated accuracy is the average of runs of 5-fold cross-validation with fixed seeds, so every reader sees the same numbers; bootstrap samples also use a fixed seed. District-level NFHS estimates of child deaths rest on relatively few births and carry wide margins of error. With 30 districts, these trees are teaching devices, not planning tools, and a split describes an association in the data, not a cause: literacy and tribal share stand in for many things that differ between districts.
