Decision Trees

A decision tree sorts districts by asking a short chain of yes-or-no questions that it learns from the data.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser
The idea in one picture

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions: it learns which questions to ask from the data, and each end box gives a prediction.

Split
One yes/no question that divides a group of districts in two.
Node
A box in the tree that asks one question.
Leaf
An end box. It gives the prediction: high or low.
Depth
How many questions in a row the tree may ask.
Impurity (Gini)
How mixed a group is: 0 means all alike, 0.5 means half and half.

Under-five mortality is the number of children who die before their fifth birthday, out of every 1,000 born alive. NFHS-5 (2019–21) measured it in every district. Half of Odisha's districts are above per 1,000. We call them high. The rest are low.

Can we sort the districts into high and low using two facts we already know? The facts are the share of women who could read in 2011 (female literacy) and the share of people from Scheduled Tribes (tribal share). A decision tree does this by asking one yes/no question at a time.

A game of yes/no questions

Think of the game where you guess something by asking yes/no questions. A good question splits the possibilities cleanly.

A tree asks a question like “Is female literacy 60% or less?”. Districts that answer yes go left. Districts that answer no go right. Then it asks again inside each group.

It starts with all districts in one box: high and low. Guessing “high” for every district would be right only of the time.

How do we score a question?

Slide a line across female literacy. At each spot, check how mixed the two sides are.

We measure mixing with Gini impurity. A group that is all one kind scores 0. A half-and-half group scores 0.5. The two sides are averaged, giving bigger sides more weight. Lower is better.

Deeper still

With three questions in a row (depth 3), the tree has leaves. Leaves are the end boxes that give the prediction. It sorts of the districts correctly.

Adding a level never lowers the score on the districts the tree learned from. It can only keep it or raise it.

Grow until every box is pure

Keep splitting until no box mixes high and low. That takes levels and leaves. Now the tree sorts of these districts correctly.

But look at the rules it needed. of its leaves hold just one district. The tree has not found a pattern. It has memorised a list.

Is it any good?

A tree tested on the districts it learned from will always look good (see Train, Test and Validation). So we test it on districts it has not seen.

We hide one-fifth of the districts, grow the tree on the rest, and test it on the hidden fifth. We do this five times, so every district is hidden once. This is called 5-fold cross-validation. We repeat it with different shuffles.

How do we stop a tree from memorising?

Two simple brakes help. A maximum depth limits how many questions the tree may ask in a row. A minimum leaf size stops it from making a rule for just one or two districts. Using limits like these is called pruning. Choose the setting by testing on unseen districts, not by training accuracy.

Can we trust one tree's rules?

A tree's first question shapes everything below it. So a small change at the top can rearrange the whole tree.

This jumpiness is why random forests exist. A random forest grows hundreds of trees, each on its own bootstrap sample, and lets them vote. The quirks of single trees tend to cancel out. The vote is much steadier than any one tree.

Remember

  1. A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions. At each step it picks the question that leaves the two groups least mixed. Its rules are easy to read.
  2. Left alone, a tree keeps splitting until it memorises the data. Limit its depth or leaf size, and choose the limit by testing on unseen districts.
  3. One tree can change shape when a single district moves. Treat its rules as a sketch, not a finding.

Data and method

Under-five mortality (deaths before age five per 1,000 live births) is from the NFHS-5 district fact sheets (2019–21); a district counts as high if its rate is above the median of the 30 districts, computed live. Female literacy, Scheduled Tribe share, urban share and population density are from Census 2011; hover a column heading in the data table for each feature's source. Trees are grown with CART: every split is a single “feature ≤ threshold” question, thresholds sit halfway between neighbouring district values, and the split chosen is the one with the lowest size-weighted Gini impurity (or entropy, where selected). Gini impurity is the chance that two districts drawn at random from a group, with replacement, carry different labels. A leaf predicts its majority label; an even split is broken towards “high”. Cross-validated accuracy is the average of runs of 5-fold cross-validation with fixed seeds, so every reader sees the same numbers; bootstrap samples also use a fixed seed. Random forests also let each split consider only a random subset of features, which makes their trees differ more. District-level NFHS estimates of child deaths rest on relatively few births and carry wide margins of error. With 30 districts, these trees are teaching devices, not planning tools, and a split describes an association in the data, not a cause: literacy and tribal share stand in for many things that differ between districts.
