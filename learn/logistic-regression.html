Logistic Regression

Some questions have a yes-or-no answer. Logistic regression turns a number into a chance between 0% and 100%. Here it is at work, flagging Odisha districts where child stunting is above the state average.

By DIU Team · Data: Census 2011 and NFHS-5 · Every number on this page is computed live in your browser

In explainer 01 we predicted a district's exact stunting rate. Planners often need something simpler: a list of districts to prioritise. So the question becomes yes or no. Is a district's child stunting at or above the Odisha average of %?

Our clue is again female literacy. We want a model that looks at a district's female literacy and says how likely a "yes" is. That model is logistic regression, one of the most widely used tools in statistics.

A straight line breaks

The obvious move is to draw a straight line through the two rows and read its height as a chance. But a line never bends.

For , where % of women read, the line gives a chance. It passes 100% for any district below % female literacy and falls below zero above %.

A chance must stay between 0% and 100%. We need a curve that bends to respect that.

Squeeze it into an S

Logistic regression keeps the straight line, but treats it as a score rather than a chance: score = b + w × literacy. The score can be any number at all.

Then the sigmoid function squeezes the score into a chance between 0 and 1:

Large positive scores give chances near 100%, large negative ones near 0%, and a score of zero gives exactly 50%. The result is the S-shaped curve on the chart.

Odds, and what the weight means

Odds are another way to say chance. A 75% chance is odds of 3 to 1: three yeses for every no. Inside the model, the score is the logarithm of the odds (the log-odds), which is why it can move in a straight line.

Scoring a curve: log-loss

How does the computer choose b and w? It scores each possible curve by how surprised it is by what actually happened. For each district, take the chance the curve gave to the true answer. The penalty is minus its logarithm.

A confident right answer costs almost nothing; a confident wrong one costs a lot. The grey lines show each district's gap to its predicted chance. The largest penalty is 's (). Averaged over all districts the fitted curve scores . Saying 50% for everyone would score .

Rolling downhill

The fitting method is called gradient descent. Start from a flat curve that says 50% for everyone. Work out which small nudge to b and w lowers the log-loss fastest, take that step, and repeat. Each pass over the districts is an epoch.

Watch the curve tilt into shape. After about epochs it stops moving, because no nudge lowers the log-loss any further.

Where to draw the line

50% is only a convention. A programme with money for more districts might flag any district with even a 30% chance, accepting more false alarms to miss fewer. A tight budget might demand 70%. Move the cut-off and watch the trade.

Adding a second clue

Logistic regression can use several clues at once. The score just gains a term per clue: score = b + w₁ × literacy + w₂ × second clue. The 50% line, where the score is zero, is now a straight line across a map.

Take-aways Logistic regression answers yes-or-no questions with a chance, not a verdict. It fits a straight-line score and squeezes it through an S-shaped curve, so the chance always stays between 0% and 100%. Each weight reads as an odds ratio: how much the odds multiply for a given step in a clue. A cut-off turns chances into decisions, and moving it trades false alarms against missed districts. Judge the model on districts it did not see, not on the ones it was fitted to. And remember that a strong association is not a cause: literacy travels with income, remoteness and access to services.

Data and method

The outcome is 1 when NFHS-5 (2019–21) stunting among children under five is at or above the state figure in the portal's Health workbook (%), else 0. Female literacy, tribal share and urban share are from Census 2011; births in a facility are from NFHS-5. Each clue is standardised (mean 0, standard deviation 1) before fitting. The model is fitted by full-batch gradient descent on log-loss (learning rate 0.5, 3,000 epochs, no penalty), and the weights are converted back to percentage points for the odds ratios. On this page w is shown per 10 points of literacy and b is centred on the average literacy. Accuracy and log-loss in the charts are measured on the same districts the model was fitted to. The cross-validated figures refit the model inside 5-fold cross-validation, repeated times with a fixed seed. Thirty districts are far too few for a planning tool, and the model shows association, not cause.
